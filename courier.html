Washington Moved. The World Moved Back.

The President ended the Iran war and turned to the Pacific. Hormuz is calmer and the economy stronger, but rivals found the gaps, allies bought insurance, and the defining crisis came out of the Baltic.

Four years ago this month, the United States was blockading Iran's ports while tankers came under attack in a half-open Strait of Hormuz. Brent crude traded near $110, gasoline had risen 34 percent in a year, Patriot interceptor stocks stood at about a third of their prewar level, and 64 percent of Americans polled called the Iran war a top voting issue.

The President chose to finish the war, hand the region's defense to Israel, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE, sell and co-produce weapons for allies who fight and pay, ask Europe to lead its own defense, and turn to the Pacific. Much of it happened. With Congress brought into the decision, talks through Oman and Qatar traded an end to the blockade for safe passage. Hormuz reopened in stages, inspectors returned to Iran's declared sites, attacks on Americans stopped, and Gulf ships took over the Hormuz patrol. Energy costs eased.

Every move drew an answer. Beijing refused rare earths to the arms makers the strategy relied on, and pressed Taiwan in ways that stayed just short of Washington's red line. In December 2028, a "volunteer" force that Moscow disowned held a town in a Baltic NATO member for 72 hours; NATO consulted under Article 4, and Washington announced no new course. Pyongyang resumed long-range missile tests. Allies carried more of the load, as asked, and also insured themselves against American delay.

By November 2030, America is out of its Gulf war, somewhat stronger at home and in its economy, and less trusted abroad. Overleaf: how each move set off the next.

Action and reaction: six chains that made the four years

Each American move set off answers that Washington did not control. Read each chain from top to bottom.

1Ending the war

The moveWith Congress brought in, seek a deal to lift the blockade, open Hormuz, and investigate the missing uranium.
The answerTehran talked through Oman and Qatar, eased harassment in stages, and traded phased inspections for relief, while keeping enrichment and rebuilding missiles underground.
ResultHormuz open, energy costs down, a top voter grievance gone. Tehran still withholds a full uranium accounting.

2Arming the allies

The moveSell, license, and co-produce American weapons for allies who fight and pay; missiles and mines for Taiwan.
The answerBeijing refused rare earths to American and Taiwanese arms makers, then to some Japanese and South Korean ones. Washington released mineral reserves and started domestic processing.
ResultFirst co-produced weapons arrive in modest numbers, held back by scarce seekers and skilled workers. Taiwan moved a step toward Washington.

3Handing off the Gulf

The moveIsrael, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE lead the region's defense; American air defenses move to Europe and Asia.
The answerRiyadh led the Hormuz mission and prepaid for American interceptors, but also spread its bets: Korean air defenses, more Pakistani troops, a hotline with Tehran, and a consent rule for using its bases against Iran.
ResultA calmer Gulf that leans on America less. In Iraq, Iran's partner factions moved toward power as American bases left.

4Europe leads

The moveEurope leads its own defense and pays for Ukraine's; America is "first among equals."
The answerSpending passed about 3.2% of GDP and Europe paid for most of Ukraine's arms, but steered new money to its own arms makers. After the raid, with no new American course, capitals planned to move first in their own groupings.
ResultA stronger, more independent Europe that wants the guarantee spelled out. Germany, France, and the Baltic states moved a step away.

5A line on Taiwan

The moveA blockade of Taiwan brings sanctions with partners, then wider sanctions and allied escorts of shipping.
The answerBeijing stayed just below the line: boardings of small Taiwan vessels near Kinmen and Matsu, each released, and yearly exercises rehearsing blockade. No ship was turned around, no approach mined, no airspace closed.
ResultThe sanctions were never triggered. The line held, and the pressure moved beneath it.

6Leaving space

The moveTurn to the Pacific, hand off the Gulf, ask Europe to lead. No initiative addressed Africa or the Global South.
The answerChina filled the space with zero-tariff access for African states, trade upgrades with Southeast Asia, and Saudi-Iranian diplomacy, while Moscow and Beijing cast American guarantees as unreliable.
ResultAmerican influence fell eight points, nearly as far as alliance cohesion. Kenya drifted toward hedging; Nigeria leaned further away.

Four lessons the four years taught

The enemy gets a vote

No rival accepted the plan as written; each answered where it would hurt. Beijing aimed its rare-earth cutoff at arms factories and its Taiwan pressure at the space just under the red line. Moscow probed NATO with a raid it could deny, then offered a ceasefire on present lines. Pyongyang drilled missile salvos against the air defenses moved to Asia. Tehran talked and rebuilt at once. Allies vote too: Riyadh set terms for its bases; Europe steered money home.

Fog of war

Who ordered the Baltic raid? Moscow denied it and called the raiders volunteers; NATO condemned the incursion but invoked Article 4, not Article 5. Where is Iran's missing uranium? No one has a full accounting. Did the Taiwan line deter Beijing, or teach it exactly where to stop? Both can be true. And silence sends a signal: when Washington announced no new course after the raid, allies read delay and planned for it.

Contingency

Washington had a plan ready for a minerals cutoff, and it bridged the gap when Beijing refused rare earths. The crisis that defined the four years came from somewhere else: a Baltic town, at the midpoint, and Washington announced no new course for it. Time is contingent too: arms promised in 2026 arrive in modest numbers in 2029 and 2030, and volume munitions, shipyards, and new mines only after 2030.

All politics is local

At home, ending the war counted most because voters ranked it near the top of their worries; cheaper fuel and, late on, factory jobs helped, while deficits and the unfinished uranium accounting fed critics. Abroad, each government answered first to its own public and industry: Riyadh tied investment to delivery dates and held normalization with Israel for a Palestinian pathway, India kept buying discounted Russian oil, and Japan kept its non-nuclear principles.
